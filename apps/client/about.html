01The problem

Multiplayer games break in the gaps between players, and those gaps are the hardest thing to test.

A single-player bug shows up when one tester presses the wrong button. A multiplayer bug only shows up when several people do something together: one player stalls while another carries the loot, someone betrays a deal at the worst moment, a griefer repeats an action in a state no designer thought of. Finding these means getting real groups of people to play the same scenario again and again, then interviewing them about what they were thinking.

ExpensiveEvery playtest needs a full lobby of humans at the same time, for every build.
SlowStudios wait days for sessions and then weeks for notes and recordings.
OpaqueFootage shows what happened. It rarely says why a player chose it.
NarrowTesters play "normally". Exploits come from the few players who don't.

01bWho it's for: the use cases

Anyone who makes a game where people play together, and can't get a room full of testers every day.

🎮 Game studios, before launch

Run hundreds of full multiplayer sessions overnight instead of booking human testers. Wake up to a list of bugs, cheats and confusing moments, each with the exact moment it happened.

Example: "The plan phase is 90 seconds too long" arrived from the speedrunner agent 18 times.

🧩 Indie teams with no testing budget

A two-person team can't fill a 6-player lobby. AI players can, and they play every night for free while you sleep.

The whole thing runs on a laptop; no API keys needed to try it.

🛡️ Finding cheats before players do

The "griefer" agent's whole job is to break the rules. It tries odd actions in odd moments, which is exactly how real cheaters find exploits.

We hid an exploit on purpose. The griefer found it 7 times without being told it existed.

⚖️ Balance checks after every patch

Change a rule, re-run the same seeds, compare the two reports. You see if betrayal became too strong or a phase got boring, before you ship.

Same seed means the same situation, so the comparison is fair.

🧭 New-player experience

The "newcomer" agent follows instructions literally and reports every time it gets stuck or confused, like a first-time player who actually tells you what went wrong.

"Nothing told me I was arrested" is a real note it filed.

🤝 Research on AI teammates

Humans and AI share one world as equals, making deals and breaking them. It's a ready-made sandbox to study trust, teamwork and deception between people and AI.

Every decision and its reason is saved, so nothing is a black box.

Why we built it

Multiplayer games are the hardest kind to test, and the people who test them rarely say why they did something. AI agents can do both: fill the seats and explain themselves. We wanted to show that AI players aren't just opponents or bots. They can be the testing team, and their reasoning is the most valuable thing they produce. A heist game is the perfect test bench because it has everything that breaks multiplayer games: teamwork, timing, money, trust and betrayal.

02The idea

Fill the empty seats with AI agents that play for real and write down why.

In Neon Bay two humans and four AI crew members, plus two AI cops, share one world as equals. Each agent has its own goal and personality, and it can lie, make deals and betray. Each agent is also a playtester with a specific testing style. Every action it takes is written to a decision ledger with the agent's own reason. When it notices a bug, exploit, confusing step or balance problem, it files an issue. The ledger then becomes a playtest report for the studio. That report is the product; the heist is the test bench.

03How it works

One authoritative server; humans and agents send the same kinds of inputs; everything lands in the ledger.

Why a server-authoritative design? If agents could edit the world directly, every report would be suspect. Here an agent is a player like any human: it sees an observation built from server state and can only send actions the server validates. Whatever the agents discover, including the exploit below, a human could also have found.

04The agent loop

Every agent repeats four steps, every 4 seconds or immediately when something happens to it.

What the agent sees

A compact observation built only from what that player could know: phase and time left, its own cash and bag, players within 40 m, speech it heard, pending deals, the result of its last action, its trust scores and its memory summary.

What it must answer

A strict JSON decision, validated with zod: {"{"} action, reason, say, trustChanges {"}"}. If the reply is invalid it gets one retry, then it falls back to wait. A slow model never freezes a character.

Why the reason matters

The reason is what turns gameplay into a test report. A studio doesn't just see "Dex laundered money six times"; it reads "Launder while still holding the bag", the agent's own words at the moment it found the exploit.

04bA game session, step by step

About 8 minutes. Here is what happens, and what gets written down, in each phase.

LOBBYuntil 2 humans ready

Everyone spawns on Second Street. Humans press R. Agents read their persona and goals.

PLAN90 s

The crew meets at the nightclub. Agents propose splits in their private BAND room. Trust starts at 50.

PREPARE120 s

Steal a car, buy stakes, scout the bank. The explorer wanders and reports dead ends.

HEIST180 s

Crack the vault (the hacker is 3× faster). The alarm rings. Cops arrive. Bags come out.

GETAWAY90 s

Race to the marina. Someone may betray a deal or tip off the police.

PAYOUT30 s

Deals settle, cash gets laundered, scores appear. The ledger is pushed to Bruin for the report.

All through the session the radio plays real headlines (Telem), the crew's private messages are mirrored to a BAND room you can watch live, and every single action from every player, human or AI, lands in the ledger with a timestamp, a position and, for agents, the reason.

05How it learns

Learning happens on four timescales. The first three belong to the agents; the fourth belongs to the studio.

LoopWhat changesWhere it livesStatus
① Every decisionTrust in each other player, nudged by what just happenedruntime/decide.ts + AgentRunner.tsBuilt
② Within a sessionThe last 30 relevant events, fed back into each observationruntime/observation.tsBuilt
③ Across sessionsA model-written summary per player, loaded at the next start; the ledger marks decisions that cite a past gameSQLite, milestone M7Planned (M7)
④ Studio loopThe game's rules, between builds, using the report as evidenceLedger → Bruin → MotherDuck → reportPipeline live

Why not train a model? These agents don't change their weights. They learn the way people at a table do: by remembering who did what and adjusting how much they trust them. That keeps every lesson readable. A memory is a sentence you can open and check, and the ledger shows exactly which decision used it. For a playtest tool, a lesson you can audit is worth more than a hidden one.

Honest status: the decision step runs through an LlmClient interface. With LLM_PROVIDER=anthropic and a key it uses Claude. Without one, as in the runs measured below, each persona runs a deterministic, observation-driven stub policy, which is also what the automated tests use. The pipeline, ledger and reports work the same either way.

07bSponsors: how each one is used, and why it fits

Each sponsor does one real job in the game. Remove any of them and the game still runs (each has a stand-in "stub"), but you lose that job.

Runware: the art department

What it is: an AI service that turns text descriptions into images, videos and 3D models.

How we use it: nearly everything you see in the bay was described in words and generated by Runware. That covers the six crew and cop characters, the street pedestrians, the dog, three sports-car paint jobs, the police station, nightclub, pawn shop, exchange and four hotel designs, the yacht, the speedboat, the lifeguard towers, the palm trees, the rooftop billboards and the photo-real wall textures.

Why it fits: the rules say "original work only, no real brands". With Runware every asset is new and made for this game from a prompt in the repo, so anyone can regenerate it. A whole 3D city cost about the price of lunch. Runware's 3D models come without skeletons, so we wrote a small auto-rigger that gives each person bones and a walk cycle.

Models
Meshy-6 (people, A-pose), Tripo v3.1 (cars, buildings, boats, props), Qwen-Image (billboards, wall photos)
In the code
packages/integrations/src/runware, pnpm assets, pnpm models

BAND: the crew's back room

What it is: a messaging platform built for AI agents, where agents and people share rooms.

How we use it: every session opens a private BAND room for the crew. Whispers, deal offers, accepted deals and betrayals are posted there as they happen. A studio watching the test can open the room and read the crew scheming in real time.

Why it fits: the most interesting part of a co-op game is what players say to each other in private. BAND gives that conversation a home outside the game, readable by humans, with each agent as a real participant. Police chatter and bribes are deliberately kept out.

Telem: the radio station's newsroom

What it is: a search service that finds fresh, real information from the web for AI apps.

How we use it: when a session starts, the server asks Telem for this week's coastal news. Every 45 seconds the in-game station, Velvet Static 96.6 FM, reads a headline. Agents hear the radio too: it's part of what they observe.

Why it fits: it makes every session a little different and grounded in the real world, which is good for players and for testing. Agents have to react to a world that changes, not a fixed script.

Turned on by
NEWS_FEED=telem
In the code
packages/integrations/src/telem

Bruin: the analyst

What it is: a data pipeline tool that moves data into a warehouse and turns it into clean, checked tables.

How we use it: when a session ends, its ledger (every action and reason) goes into a MotherDuck warehouse. A Bruin pipeline, running hourly in Bruin Cloud, builds the report tables: time per phase, issues by type, where players got stuck, the most common winning action sequences, and a position heat map. It checks the data with 17 quality checks.

Why it fits: the playtest report is the product. Bruin turns thousands of raw decisions into a few tables a designer can read, every hour, without anyone running scripts by hand.

Turned on by
LEDGER_SINK=bruin
In the code
packages/integrations/src/bruin, pipelines/neon-bay-ledger
Sponsors we did not use, and why

Harness Router needed a separate model provider key before it would run any task, so we dropped it. Finch, Glasser, Casuro and Teamily were dropped because we had no keys for them. Every one of those features still has a stub, so they can be added later without changing the game.

09Words used on this page

Agent
An AI player. It looks at the game, decides what to do, and does it, just like a person with a controller.
Persona
The agent's personality and testing style: speedrunner, explorer, griefer or newcomer.
Ledger
The diary of the session: one line per action, saying who did what, where, when and why.
Playtest report
The summary a studio reads: issues, stuck points, winning strategies and a heat map, built from the ledger.
Server-authoritative
The game server is the referee. Players and agents can only ask to do things; the server decides what really happens.
Stub
A stand-in for an outside service, so the game still works with no internet and no keys.
Trust score
A number from 0 to 100 each agent keeps about every other player. Betrayals push it down, kept promises push it up.
Seed
A number that fixes the random choices, so a session can be replayed in exactly the same situation.

Common questions

Are the AI players cheating?

No. They only see what a player standing there could see, and every action goes through the same server rules as a human's. If an agent finds an exploit, a human could too, which is the point.

Do I need API keys to try it?

No. Every outside service has a stub. Run pnpm dev and the whole game, agents included, works offline. Keys switch on the real services one by one.

What did the art cost?

All Runware images and 3D models together cost a few tens of dollars at most. Each asset's price is recorded in its manifest file. Re-running the scripts never pays twice for an asset that already exists.

Do the agents really learn?

They learn the way people at a table do: they remember who did what and adjust how much they trust each other. Carrying memories across sessions is the next milestone. Nothing is hidden: every memory is a readable sentence.

Are the cars, buildings and people copied from anywhere?

No. Every model was generated for this game from our own text descriptions, with no real brands, car makes, logos or characters. The few downloaded assets (sky, ground textures, lamps and bins) are public domain (CC0) and listed in ASSETS.md.
