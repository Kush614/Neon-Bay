01The problem

Multiplayer games break in the gaps between players, and those gaps are the hardest thing to test.

A single-player bug shows up when one tester presses the wrong button. A multiplayer bug only shows up when several people do something together: one player stalls while another carries the loot, someone betrays a deal at the worst moment, a griefer repeats an action in a state no designer thought of. Finding these means getting real groups of people to play the same scenario again and again, then interviewing them about what they were thinking.

ExpensiveEvery playtest needs a full lobby of humans at the same time, for every build.
SlowStudios wait days for sessions and then weeks for notes and recordings.
OpaqueFootage shows what happened. It rarely says why a player chose it.
NarrowTesters play "normally". Exploits come from the few players who don't.

02The idea

Fill the empty seats with AI agents that play for real and write down why.

In Neon Bay two humans and four AI crew members, plus two AI cops, share one world as equals. Each agent has its own goal and personality, and it can lie, make deals and betray. Each agent is also a playtester with a specific testing style. Every action it takes is written to a decision ledger with the agent's own reason. When it notices a bug, exploit, confusing step or balance problem, it files an issue. The ledger then becomes a playtest report for the studio. That report is the product; the heist is the test bench.

03How it works

One authoritative server; humans and agents send the same kinds of inputs; everything lands in the ledger.

Why a server-authoritative design? If agents could edit the world directly, every report would be suspect. Here an agent is a player like any human: it sees an observation built from server state and can only send actions the server validates. Whatever the agents discover, including the exploit below, a human could also have found.

04The agent loop

Every agent repeats four steps, every 4 seconds or immediately when something happens to it.

What the agent sees

A compact observation built only from what that player could know: phase and time left, its own cash and bag, players within 40 m, speech it heard, pending deals, the result of its last action, its trust scores and its memory summary.

What it must answer

A strict JSON decision, validated with zod: {"{"} action, reason, say, trustChanges {"}"}. If the reply is invalid it gets one retry, then it falls back to wait. A slow model never freezes a character.

Why the reason matters

The reason is what turns gameplay into a test report. A studio doesn't just see "Dex laundered money six times"; it reads "Launder while still holding the bag", the agent's own words at the moment it found the exploit.

05How it learns

Learning happens on four timescales. The first three belong to the agents; the fourth belongs to the studio.

LoopWhat changesWhere it livesStatus
① Every decisionTrust in each other player, nudged by what just happenedruntime/decide.ts + AgentRunner.tsBuilt
② Within a sessionThe last 30 relevant events, fed back into each observationruntime/observation.tsBuilt
③ Across sessionsA model-written summary per player, loaded at the next start; the ledger marks decisions that cite a past gameSQLite, milestone M7Planned (M7)
④ Studio loopThe game's rules, between builds, using the report as evidenceLedger → Bruin → MotherDuck → reportPipeline live

Why not train a model? These agents don't change their weights. They learn the way people at a table do: by remembering who did what and adjusting how much they trust them. That keeps every lesson readable. A memory is a sentence you can open and check, and the ledger shows exactly which decision used it. For a playtest tool, a lesson you can audit is worth more than a hidden one.

Honest status: the decision step runs through an LlmClient interface. With LLM_PROVIDER=anthropic and a key it uses Claude. Without one, as in the runs measured below, each persona runs a deterministic, observation-driven stub policy, which is also what the automated tests use. The pipeline, ledger and reports work the same either way.

From 21 recorded sessions: 19,477 decisions and 48 issues the agents filed themselves.

Each persona found the kind of problem it was built to find. These are real entries from the ledger:

"Laundering while carrying a bag credits the clean cash but never deducts the dirty cash, so repeating launder…"Dex · griefer · exploit · high. This is the exploit planted in the design on purpose to check that the testers would find it. They did, without being told.
"One player abusing the laundering exploit pushed clean cash past 600,000 and crashed every stake price…"Dex · balance · high. A world-wide market crash caused by agents alone.
"The plan phase runs 90 s even when the crew has nothing left to agree; a driver just stands at the nightclub."Rico · speedrunner · balance · low. A pacing note a designer can act on directly.
"I tried move_to and was told 'you are arrested and cannot act'. Nothing told me that beforehand."Opal · newcomer · confusing · low. Missing feedback, the kind of note that usually needs a user interview.
